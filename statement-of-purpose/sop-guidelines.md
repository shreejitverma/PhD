Here's the full framework. Your four items are a good start, but two of them need reframing, and the list is missing the components that actually decide funded admissions. First, one piece of context that shapes everything: at research universities, a PhD SOP is read by faculty asking a single question — *"Do I want to fund and advise this person for five years, and will they produce research?"* Funding usually isn't a separate negotiation; admission and funding travel together, and the *best* packages (fellowships, top RA-ships) go to applicants a professor decides to champion. So every component below serves one goal: create a champion.

**The ten components, in the order they should appear**

1. **A motivating research question, not a life story.** Open with the intellectual problem that drives you, using personal history only as fuel for it. In your drafts, the insurance story exists solely to set up "liquidity vanishes when it matters most" — two sentences of biography, then the question.

2. **Proof you've completed a full research cycle.** This is the single most-weighted item, and it's why your thesis gets two paragraphs: question → method → result → *limitation* → next question. Committees fund people who've already done research, not people who want to try it.

3. **Stated limitations of your own work.** Counterintuitive but critical: naming the PCIe saturation, the LUT/DSP tension, the sim-to-real gap signals research maturity. Applicants who present only wins read as students; applicants who know where their work breaks read as colleagues.

4. **Research breadth with epistemic honesty.** Secondary projects show range, but frame results skeptically — "a 155% backtest I learned to distrust" impresses professors more than the 155% does.

5. **A concrete research agenda: 2–3 specific directions.** This is the biggest gap in most applications and the strongest funding lever, because it lets a professor see their next funded paper in you. It's not a binding contract — everyone knows plans change — it's evidence you generate researchable questions.

6. **Named-faculty fit with real hooks.** Not "your eminent faculty" but "Uryasev co-invented the risk measure my framework already optimizes." Two or three professors, each tied to a specific front of your agenda. This paragraph determines who champions you in the committee room.

7. **Explanation of anomalies, briefly and without drama.** Your CMU withdrawal gets one sentence framed as fact plus resilience. Unexplained gaps invite doubt; over-explained ones invite pity. Neither gets funded.

8. **What you give the department, not just take from it.** Teaching evidence (your 2,500-person BofA sessions, SGFA presidency) matters for TA funding specifically, and tools or systems others can use (your FPGA testbed) matter for lab value.

9. **Preparation woven in, never listed.** GPA, coursework, and skills appear in one compact sentence in service of the agenda — the transcript already exists; don't recite it.

10. **Research-career goals.** State professor or industrial research lab. Never end a PhD SOP with "become a quant at a hedge fund" — faculty fund future researchers, and a stated industry-job goal can quietly kill fellowship nominations even in applied programs.

**Where your four items fit — and two corrections**

Your #1 (capability) maps to components 2–4 and 9, with the weighting research > academics > work experience; industry work only counts insofar as it sharpened research questions, which is exactly how your BNP paragraph is framed. Your #4 (goals) is component 10. But #2 needs inverting: "what you'll learn from the program" is master's-application language. A PhD SOP emphasizes what you'll *produce* — a master's student consumes a curriculum, a PhD student generates knowledge, and funding follows producers. And #3 is a real thing done the wrong way if written as "I have sponsorship": you never claim support in the SOP. Instead, you email professors *before* applying (short note, CV, one-paragraph pitch referencing their specific paper, ask if they're taking students), and if the exchange goes well, one truthful line — "after corresponding with Prof. X…" — goes in the fit paragraph. For Stevens you're the rare case where the relationship already exists, and Bozdog's letter will matter more than the SOP itself.

**Funding levers that live outside the SOP**

Since maximizing the stipend is the goal: apply by the funding-consideration deadline (Stony Brook's is December 15), and check every fellowship-nomination box on the application. Align your proposed agenda with professors' *active grants* — Yang's NSF-funded CRAFT center is a live example, and you can look up any professor's awards on the NSF award search to aim your fit paragraph at funded work, which is how RA-ships get created for specific applicants. Note that NSF GRFP is restricted to US citizens and permanent residents, so if that excludes you, university fellowships and grant-funded RA positions are the game. And recommendation letters outrank the SOP for funding decisions — a letter saying "I would take this student into my lab tomorrow" is the strongest stipend negotiation that exists.
